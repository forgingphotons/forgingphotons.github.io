Sprawling elegantly across the autumn sky, Messier 33—commonly known as the Triangulum Galaxy—is the third-largest member of our Local Group of galaxies, trailing only Andromeda and our own Milky Way. Face-on to our line of sight, M33 reveals a stunning tapestry of loosely wound spiral arms adorned with glowing pink emission nebulae and clusters of brilliant blue young stars.

Among these star-forming regions lies NGC 604, one of the largest known stellar nurseries in the entire Local Group. This colossal cloud of ionized hydrogen is over 40 times larger than the famous Orion Nebula and produces stars so furiously that, if it were as close to Earth as Orion, it would outshine Venus in our night sky.

Despite containing roughly 40 billion stars, M33 lacks a supermassive black hole at its core or a prominent central bulge. Instead, it is dynamically bound to the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), engaging in a gravitational dance that will eventually lead to a dramatic collision and merger billions of years in the future.
