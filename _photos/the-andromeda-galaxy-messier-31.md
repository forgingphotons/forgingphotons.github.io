Shining as our nearest major galactic neighbor, Messier 31 (M31)—universally known as the Andromeda Galaxy—spans over 200,000 light-years across, containing upwards of a trillion stars. Sprawling across the autumn night sky, this majestic spiral galaxy is visible even to the naked eye under dark conditions, appearing as a faint, elongated oval of ancient starlight.

Telescopic imaging reveals Andromeda in breathtaking detail: a brilliant, golden core densely packed with old stars, surrounded by sweeping spiral arms laced with intricate dust lanes. Bright blue associations of young, massive stars trace the outer edges of the arms alongside glowing pink star-forming H II regions, highlighting active pockets of stellar birth. Accompanying M31 in its gravitational orbit are its two prominent satellite galaxies, M32 and M110, visible as small, glowing elliptical companions against the cosmic backdrop.

As the most massive member of our Local Group of galaxies, Andromeda is currently hurtling toward our Milky Way under mutual gravitational attraction, destined to merge in a grand cosmic collision billions of years in the future.
