Resembling a delicate blossom blooming in the cosmic dark, the Iris Nebula (NGC 7023) is one of the most famous and striking reflection nebulae in the northern sky. Unlike emission nebulae that glow red from ionized gas, the striking deep blue color of the Iris comes from starlight scattering off fine grains of interstellar dust—the exact same physical process that makes Earth's sky appear blue.

At the core of the flower sits HD 200775, a massive, hot blue star that illuminates the surrounding cloud. The intense radiation from this central star carves out a hollow cavity in the dust cloud, revealing complex filaments and glowing gossamer structures that resemble delicate petals spread across six light-years of space.

Surrounding the bright blue central blossom is a dense, murky sea of dark dust clouds (cataloged as *LDN 1147* and *LDN 1158*). These opaque, brownish clouds contain complex organic molecules known as Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs). When exposed to the ultraviolet radiation of the central star, these complex carbon-based molecules fluoresce in infrared wavelengths, making the Iris Nebula a key laboratory for studying the cosmic chemistry that forms the building blocks of planets.
