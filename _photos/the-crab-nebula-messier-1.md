The Crab Nebula (Messier 1) is one of the most famous and intensely studied objects in astronomy: the expanding debris cloud of a massive stellar explosion. Unlike most deep-sky mysteries, humanity actually witnessed its birth. In the year 1054 AD, Chinese, Japanese, Arab, and Native American astronomers recorded a brilliant "guest star" in the night sky that was bright enough to be seen in broad daylight for weeks and visible at night for nearly two years.

Centuries later, telescopes revealed a complex, glowing web of crimson filaments laced with eerie, ghostly blue light. The crimson outer shroud is expanding outward at thousands of kilometers per second, consisting of the original star's outer layers heated by the blast wave.

The true engine driving this cosmic masterpiece lies right at its center: a pulsar, which is the ultra-dense, city-sized spinning corpse of the original collapsed star. Weighing more than our Sun compressed into a sphere just 20 kilometers wide, this neutron star rotates thirty times every second, beaming intense electromagnetic radiation across space and energizing the entire nebula into a glowing powerhouse of high-energy physics.
